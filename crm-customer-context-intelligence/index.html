The Changing Face of CRM

How workflows and intelligence are replicable, but here's what isn't.

By Aditya Vithaldas

What this is: independent research from Frontier Labs, not client work. Part one of an essay series.

You must have seen the wave of people on X and online saying, "I could rebuild my CRM in a weekend." Clearly that's an exaggeration, but there is some truth to it: the workflow dimension of CRM is a lot more replicable than it used to be.

And if you noticed, last week HubSpot co-founder Brian Halligan came back as CEO and announced that he and Dharmesh Shah are re-founding the company as the "Customer Harness Company." They can see it too: with the workflow part replicable, they need to give something more.

There's one more pattern. A huge number of startups are moving in the direction of social media listening and social media outreach, beyond the scope of what is traditionally CRM. One interesting example comes from Dan Koe, one of the most popular posters on X, who talks about reaching his audience using Eden, a tool he conceptualised and uses successfully. In his words, it's "everything I learned, built into the tool I wish I had when I started."

So what does this mean for CRM? Go back to the 2024–25 wave. CRM meant drip campaigns and optimising how and where best to reach a customer. Then, with LLMs coming to the fore, it evolved into automated proposal building and automated responses, which you could use in HubSpot, Salesforce and the other typical tools. Anthropic's sales leaders, for example, talk about how Claude already does a significant amount of the grunt work on their proposals. Is that CRM? Maybe so, but augmenting creation is also a huge lever.

MCP and CRM as a source of record

So where do we stand at the end of 2026, and what can companies compete on? MCPs are pretty much a must-have for every SaaS operation, and a CRM is no different. Customers being able to trigger actions through whatever agents they use, and drive outcomes, is a necessity. But does that also risk turning these firms into a dumb pipe?

If what a customer wants is predominantly workflow they configure, can they not do it themselves? And if they do, they may build their own set of skills that bypass any AI features the CRM offers. So clearly that cannot be it. It isn't just about configuration and flexibility; it is about a lot more. What is that lot more? Let's zoom out and step back into the overall CRM workflow.

Acquisition

Start with customer acquisition. Traditionally, the CRM's entry point was a contact or a lead. Can the CRM do more? If there's one thing existing customer workflows cannot do, it's reaching a customer base that doesn't yet exist in your system. Is there a role there? This is where an interesting set of people are innovating. Context.dev, for example, pitches its people search API as "1 billion people, one API", letting you reach anybody and target on any topic. And Dan Koe's Eden lets you go after groups and find posts, whether on X, Facebook or Reddit, that become a huge source of potential customers, even though they aren't named yet.

So is outreach and action in a group also a core part of what a CRM should be? It's clearly harder, and a more defensible moat, than pure workflow, so it's definitely worth a shot. I genuinely believe that acquisition through communities and groups, and outreach to a customer even before they become a named customer in your system, becomes an essential part of the portfolio. Maybe the CRM players have already built some of it, but it is now absolutely essential to get going.

Channel

Now you have the contact. Second, we get to channel. Sure, you know the customer, but how do you actually reach them? If you find it so easy to reach out to a million customers, surely the entire world can do that too. So what does that mean for the future of customer outreach?

Start with email. There have been murmurs of users claiming that email is dead, Dan Shipper's post among them. Is it really dead? All of us have observed more spam coming through our inboxes, and while email is still largely dependable for most customers, it's obvious it is going to get heavily polluted over the coming months. Email has always been the holy grail of outreach. It may be too early to call it, but clearly email is no longer a channel where you can be assured you'll be read.

In parallel, coming back to the social point above, LinkedIn and the rest are getting more and more noisy too. Attention is the new currency. How can a CRM player ensure not just that people can reach their audience, but that they can gain that audience's attention?

What are the attention sources? Clearly micro-influencers start playing a larger role in the overall equation, as do people who already write to a set audience. If a commercial user is going to read only a few notes, they're likely to come from someone they already follow. Wispr Flow is a good example: Hector Serrano, their Head of Content, talked on Lenny's Podcast about working with around 50 creators.

So how does a company make sure its customers are paying attention, that they're listening to it? Is it going to be about the CRM, or a lot more? Having the right hook, both directly and through channels, both at the time of purchase and on a continual basis, becomes key. Does every company become a pseudo media company? Maybe not, but at the very least every company needs to be a trusted player that won't be ignored into oblivion. That's clearly a problem to be solved.

And there's another key dimension: your audience on the channel is going to be agents. They're the ones searching for decisions and for tools at a particular point in time, doing the detailed evaluation needed to make a choice a success. How do you make sure you're worthy of that? How do you make sure coding agents, ChatGPT and Claude Code are all able to make sense of choosing you when the time comes to be chosen?

How can a CRM ensure that companies solve this?

This is part one. In the next post, we'll talk about context and intelligence, and how you can go down the path of building out the entire solution: not just one that can be vibe-coded, but one that is truly sustainable, or at least more so than what exists right now, and one that's impossible for someone to just build out in the short term.
